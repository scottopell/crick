# Creek Game — Vision

> Working vision for a human-scale, idle creek-tending game. The project does not yet have a final title.

## One\-sentence vision

Build an enjoyable personal creek—swimming holes, crossings, beaches, cascades, and side channels—through small physical interventions, then leave and discover how flowing water transformed the work over time\.

## Product fantasy

The player owns or tends a modest creekside property\. They stand in the creek at human scale, read the current, handle individual rocks, shovel gravel, and gradually undertake larger projects that one determined person could plausibly complete\.

The player does not sculpt terrain as an omnipotent river god\. They make suggestions to the creek\. Water supplies the vastly greater force: scouring pools, sorting sediment, exposing useful stone, filling failed channels, dismantling structures, and sometimes improving an intervention in an unexpected way\.

The fantasy is not controlling nature perfectly\. It is becoming remarkably good at collaborating with a particular creek\.

## Core design thesis

**Specify the function, not the structure\.**

Projects define experiences the property should provide: a usable swimming hole, a passable crossing, a gravel beach, an attractive cascade, or a flowing side channel\. The game never requires one prescribed arrangement of rocks\.

A successful creek should not remain visually unchanged\. It should continue evolving while preserving—or naturally re\-forming—the functions the player cares about\. A design that moves, settles, and repairs itself can be better than a rigid structure that merely resists change\.

## Vision pillars

### 1\. Human\-scale physicality

- The world is experienced from within one intimate creek reach, not from a watershed map\.
- Rocks have believable size, weight, shape, friction, and fit\.
- The player carries cobbles, drags awkward stones, shovels gravel, and uses simple leverage\.
- Large projects require repeated sessions, accumulated labor, and cooperation from the creek\.
- Player capability remains recognizably human; progression does not become superhuman earthmoving\.

### 2\. Water is the idle engine

- The player positions material; continuous flow performs work\.
- Ordinary flow creates gradual, directional change\.
- Rain and smaller high\-flow events reveal weaknesses and accelerate channel formation\.
- Seasonal floods punctuate the long arc and test the player’s accumulated work\.
- While the player is away, their body rests, the creek transports material, and every feature accumulates history\.

### 3\. Functional goals, emergent solutions

- Each project has measurable operating conditions rather than an authored solution\.
- Geometry alone is not success: a pool must remain usable, a crossing must remain passable, and a side channel must carry useful flow\.
- Multiple designs can succeed with different costs, maintenance burdens, and side effects\.
- Local success can create downstream problems, so projects become increasingly interconnected\.

### 4\. Evolution is visible and desirable

- Erosion, deposition, settling, collapse, and redirection are core feedback—not cosmetic decay\.
- Failure must be active and legible\. The creek dismantling or redirecting a design is interesting; nothing happening without an understandable reason is not\.
- Old attempts remain visible as displaced rocks, abandoned cuts, buried structures, and inherited channel forms\.
- The property becomes a record of the player’s decisions rather than a sequence of clean resets\.

### 5\. Observation is play

- Reading depth, velocity, current lines, sediment, and rock movement is as important as construction\.
- The simulation must communicate cause and effect without requiring formal hydraulic training\.
- The player should develop useful intuitive theories about water, test them, and revise them\.
- Accelerated playback and timeline inspection exist to make slow processes understandable, not merely to make numbers rise faster\.

## Core loop

1. **Inspect:** Walk the creek and notice current, depth, erosion, deposition, and available material\.
2. **Choose:** Select a personal\-property feature or maintenance problem to work toward\.
3. **Intervene:** Move rocks, shovel gravel, cut a pilot channel, or repair an existing structure within the available labor budget\.
4. **Observe:** Watch the immediate hydraulic response and optionally accelerate time to test an idea\.
5. **Leave:** Ordinary flow continues working while the player is away\.
6. **Return:** See the changed creek first, then scrub backward through elapsed time to discover how it happened\.
7. **Adapt:** Continue from the altered terrain\. Rebuild, salvage, reinforce, or take advantage of what the creek created\.

## The personal creek

The initial world should be one compact, richly simulated creek reach—approximately tens of meters, not kilometers\. It contains several naturally promising sites but no predetermined construction footprints\.

The long\-term objective is to make this reach increasingly enjoyable to inhabit while retaining a natural appearance and dynamic behavior\. Progress comes from a growing portfolio of functional features, not from replacing the creek with permanent masonry\.

### Candidate v1 features

#### Swimming hole

Create and maintain a meaningful volume of sufficiently deep, relatively calm water with a usable entry and exit\. The pool may be sustained by induced scour rather than manual excavation alone\. Sediment infill, migrating current, and failure of the downstream control are continuing pressures\.

#### Stepping\-stone crossing

Create a comfortable route across the creek during ordinary flow\. Stone spacing, footing stability, submergence, backwater, and scour around each base determine its usefulness over time\.

#### Gravel beach or wading shelf

Encourage coarse sediment to accumulate into an accessible, gently sloped shoreline beside calm water\. The challenge is not simply creating deposition, but obtaining the right material in the right place without it becoming a mud trap or migrating away\.

#### Cascade or listening spot

Shape a visually and acoustically pleasing drop near a place to sit\. Its usefulness depends on how flow distributes at different water levels, whether downstream scour remains constructive, and whether low flow continues to use the intended path\.

#### Side channel and island

Excavate a pilot path and persuade the creek to complete it\. Success requires useful flow across varying conditions without the entrance plugging, the channel drying permanently, or the new route capturing the entire creek\. This is a natural multi\-week project\.

#### Protected access

Keep a path, sitting bank, or small area of property comfortably accessible despite erosion\. A brute\-force solution that merely transfers erosion across or downstream is incomplete\.

Physical habitat can be represented in v1 as a hydraulic or geomorphic property of features\. Plants, animals, and ecological outcomes are a deliberate later expansion\.

## Measuring success

The game should not collapse the creek into one universal health or beauty score\. It should track whether each chosen feature is providing its intended function across time and changing flow\.

The primary candidate measure is **feature uptime**:

- Swimming\-hole days
- Passable\-crossing days
- Usable\-beach days
- Side\-channel connected days
- Protected\-access days

Each feature can mature through four legible states:

1. **Created:** It satisfies its functional range now\.
2. **Established:** It continues functioning through ordinary flow variation\.
3. **Proven:** It remains functional—or naturally reforms—after a significant flood\.
4. **Self\-maintaining:** It persists for an extended period with little corrective labor\.

Performance can also reflect:

- Player labor invested
- Imported versus creek\-delivered material
- Maintenance frequency
- Unintended erosion or deposition elsewhere
- Recovery time after a disruptive event
- Range of flows over which the feature remains useful

These measurements should explain outcomes, not dictate aesthetics or imply that all change is damage\.

## Progression

Progression should primarily increase the ambition and interaction of projects rather than the player’s raw strength\.

Early work may create a basic crossing or shallow wading area\. Later work can establish a true swimming hole, cultivate a gravel beach, open a side channel, and eventually coordinate an entire sequence of pools, riffles, access points, and divided flows\.

The limiting resources currently established are:

- **Physical energy and work time**, which recover while the player is away\.
- **Available material**, much of which the creek exposes, sorts, or delivers over time\.

As the property becomes more developed, maintaining several interacting features becomes part of play\. An upstream intervention may starve a downstream beach of gravel; a side channel may relieve pressure on one bank while changing the swimming hole’s depth\.

## Time, weather, and floods

Time must operate at several readable scales:

- Immediate current response after moving a rock
- Minutes or hours of accelerated scour and deposition
- Days of ordinary idle evolution
- Rain\-driven events that produce meaningful rearrangement
- Seasonal floods that test the full property

On return, the player sees the current result before receiving an explanation\. They can then scrub backward through a compressed simulation history, inspect important moments, and understand where material moved and why\.

A damaging flood never resets the level\. The player continues on the altered creek, recovering scattered material and building from what remains\. Floods create chapters in one persistent landscape\.

## Simulation promise

The simulation does not need academically complete computational fluid dynamics\. It does need consistent, convincing relationships:

- Constriction generally increases velocity\.
- Fast water scours movable beds and banks\.
- Slow water deposits transported material\.
- Rock size, shape, embedment, support, and local flow affect mobility\.
- Large obstacles create wakes, eddies, and downstream deposition\.
- Small low points can capture flow and enlarge over time\.
- Sediment sorts by size as transport capacity changes\.
- Structures alter conditions both upstream and downstream\.
- High\-flow events access normally dry areas and move larger material\.

The player must be able to see evidence for these rules and develop reliable intuition, even when individual outcomes remain surprising\.

## Tone and presentation

The desired tone sits between a garden game, a terrarium, a tactile physics toy, and a quiet engineering simulation\.

Key sensory material includes wet stone, gravel shifting underfoot, muddy water clearing, leaves caught in eddies, changing water sound, cold shallow flow, and the visual revelation of current lines\. The creek should invite lingering and close inspection\.

The interface should avoid making the creek feel like an industrial dashboard\. Measurements may be available through natural tools, overlays, field notes, stakes, gauges, and the timeline, while the default view remains grounded in the place itself\.

## Explicit non\-goals

- Not a god game operating on an entire river system\.
- Not a city builder or watershed\-management map\.
- Not a traditional puzzle with one correct rock arrangement\.
- Not a static diorama where completed structures remain permanently unchanged\.
- Not an exponential strength fantasy ending in heavy industrial equipment\.
- Not initially an ecosystem collector centered on unlocking plants and animals\.
- Not a rigorous professional engineering package requiring expert knowledge\.
- Not a currency machine with coins visibly produced by water features\.

## Prototype question

The first prototype should answer one question:

> Is it satisfying to move a few believable rocks, watch the current respond, leave the creek running, and return to a changed but understandable result?

A useful vertical slice needs only:

- One short creek reach
- A credible distribution of gravel, cobbles, and several immovable rocks
- Picking up, rotating, dragging, and placing stones
- Basic gravel excavation or shoveling
- Readable velocity, erosion, transport, and deposition
- One swimming\-hole objective and one stepping\-stone objective
- Accelerated simulation
- Return\-to\-present reveal followed by backward timeline scrubbing
- One high\-flow event
- Persistent aftermath with no reset

If manipulating and reading the creek is not intrinsically pleasurable, additional progression systems will not rescue the concept\.

## Open design questions

- What funds or authorizes new property projects, if anything beyond player choice?
- Is feature uptime itself the progression currency, or only a record of accomplishment?
- How much active\-session time should pass before the player benefits from leaving?
- How are hydraulic measurements surfaced without replacing observation?
- Does the player inhabit an embodied first\-person character, a close third\-person character, or an intimate tool\-level camera?
- How much work can the creek perform during offline time without making results feel arbitrary?
- Which failure causes are essential for the first simulation, and which can wait?
- What is the minimum sediment model capable of producing convincing self\-excavating pools, bars, and side channels?

## North star

**A personal creek that becomes more useful, more storied, and more distinctly yours—not because it stops changing, but because your work continues to function through its changes\.**
